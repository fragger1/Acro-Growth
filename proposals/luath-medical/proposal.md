## Screening answers

### 1. What experience do you have within the MedTech industry?

> None directly in medical devices yet, so I can't point you to MedTech campaign results.
>
> The closest work is outbound list building for a client whose targets include machine shops, metal fabricators, plastics fabricators and industrial equipment suppliers: technical manufacturing buyers, not SaaS.
>
> For this application I've mapped where your two buyer types can be found and qualified: the FDA 510(k) database, ClinicalTrials.gov, Enterprise Ireland/EIC/SBIR awards, engineering job posts, Irish Medtech Association members and trade-show exhibitor lists. That's in the attached plan. With your case studies and messaging, I'll have copy for both segments ready for your review in the first week, and you sign off before anything sends.

### 2. Describe a B2B list-building or targeting approach you've used for a niche, technical industry (not general SaaS/e-commerce). What signals did you use to qualify a good-fit company?

> For a client targeting small manufacturers (machine shops, metal fabricators, plastics fabricators, industrial equipment suppliers) alongside other local businesses, I built a pipeline that searches Google Maps by keyword across about 60 cities in their territory, crawls each business's website for contact emails, and stores everything in a database for export to cold email.
>
> Three signals qualified a good-fit company:
> 1. **The primary category matches the target.** Maps pads results with loosely related places once real matches run out (a "machine shop" search returns crypto ATMs and gas stations). A lead only passes if its primary Maps category matches the keyword or a mapped equivalent: "Tool & die shop" and "Mold maker" count for "machine shop".
> 2. **It's inside the service area.** It has to be within a 40-mile radius of the client's base. Checking by coordinates also covers the roughly 1 in 5 places Maps lists with no address.
> 3. **It has a real, reachable contact.** That means a working domain and a business email. Junk is filtered out, such as addresses that appear on several unrelated websites (web-designer and font-vendor emails buried in site code) and home-ISP inboxes.
>
> For Luath I'd swap in MedTech signals. For startups: a funding round or grant in the last 12 months, 2–30 staff, few or no mechanical engineers, and no clearance yet. For OEMs: medical device NAICS codes (339112, 339113, 334510), 50+ staff, long-open mechanical/R&D engineering roles, and recent 510(k) clearances or launches.

### 3. We have two different buyer types — early-stage startup founders and OEM engineering managers. How would your outreach approach differ between them?

> They differ in trigger, message, proof, ask and cadence. The infrastructure underneath is shared.
>
> **Startup founders.** The trigger is time pressure: a raise just closed, or bench testing or a round is coming up. The pain is speed and cash. They need a prototype but can't justify a full-time hire. Emails are short and founder to founder, the proof is concept-to-prototype stories, and the ask is a 15-minute call about their timeline. The cadence is tighter, about 4 touches over 2 weeks, paired with LinkedIn, where founders are active.
>
> **OEM engineering managers.** The trigger is capacity: long-open roles, launches, line transfers. The pain is a stretched team that can't add headcount. Emails are engineer to engineer and specific about deliverables (DFM, tolerance stack-ups, design reviews, tooling), with no hype. The ask is low commitment: "Want a one-page overview?" The cadence is longer, about 5 touches over 4 weeks, and multi-threaded: a second contact on the same team, plus LinkedIn Matched Audience ads to the same accounts.
>
> **Shared underneath:** one Apollo workspace and sending setup, one LinkedIn presence, your case studies, and one weekly report split by segment, so you can see which one turns into meetings.

### 4. Tell us about an Apollo or LinkedIn campaign that underperformed — what did you diagnose as the cause, and what did you change?

> The closest real example I have is a list problem rather than an Apollo or LinkedIn campaign, so I'll be upfront about that.
>
> For the manufacturing-focused client above, the first exports had healthy volume but included a lot of off-target businesses. I found two causes. First, Google Maps pads searches with unrelated places once the real matches run out. Second, my first filter matched on any of a business's categories, and about 600 leads only got through on a secondary category: shipping stores tagged "Print shop", paint stores, insurance agencies.
>
> The fix: I switched to matching on the primary category only, added a mapping table for legitimate category variants, and added a service-area radius. The export now logs everything it skips, so I can review it rather than trust it blindly.
>
> The lesson I apply to every campaign: when results disappoint, check the list before rewriting the copy.

*(If you have a real Apollo or LinkedIn campaign that underperformed, with what you changed and the before/after numbers, replace this answer with it. It's what the client is really asking for.)*

### 5. How do you decide what tone of voice to write in?

> I write in the buyer's voice, not the seller's. Three inputs decide it:
> 1. **Who's reading.** Founders get short, direct, peer-to-peer emails about their timeline. Engineering managers get precise, technical, low-pressure emails that read like a scope.
> 2. **How they talk.** I read their LinkedIn posts, job ads and conference talks and borrow their vocabulary ("design freeze", "V&V", "stack-up") instead of marketing language.
> 3. **Your brand.** Your case studies, your website and how your team talks on calls, so a prospect who replies hears the same voice on the first call.
>
> Then the data settles it: I test two angles per segment and keep what gets positive replies. The default for both of your audiences: plain English, specific, one ask per email, no hype.
